Unit 3: Foundations of Cell Theory

A detailed breakdown of biogenesis, historical paradigm shifts, and core principles in cellular biology.

Unit 3 Refutation of Spontaneous Generation (Abiogenesis)

For centuries, classical biology was dominated by the concept of spontaneous generation—the belief that living organisms could arise spontaneously from non-living matter[cite: 79]. Controlled experimentation systematically disproved this theory:

  • Abiogenesis Premise: Supposed that decaying organic material could directly generate living organisms (e.g., maggots from decaying meat) without biological parentage[cite: 79].
  • Francesco Redi (1668): Performed controlled trials using three jars containing meat (uncovered, airtight sealed, and fine gauze covered)[cite: 79]. Maggots only developed in open jars where flies could lay eggs, establishing that macroscopic life does not spontaneously generate[cite: 79].
  • Louis Pasteur (1861): Provided definitive proof against abiogenesis using swan-necked flasks[cite: 79, 80]. Nutrient broth was sterilized via boiling; the curved neck allowed free oxygen exchange while trapping airborne dust particles and spores in the bend[cite: 80]. The broth remained sterile indefinitely until the neck was severed, demonstrating that micro-organisms arise solely from pre-existing airborne microbes[cite: 79, 80].

Unit 3 Core Postulates of Modern Cell Theory

  • 1. Universal Cellular Composition: All living organisms—ranging from single-celled prokaryotes to complex multicellular eukaryotes—are composed of one or more cells[cite: 8, 9, 15].
  • 2. Primary Structural & Functional Unit: The cell represents the structural baseline and functional unit of biological organization capable of autonomous metabolic activity[cite: 8, 9, 15].
  • 3. Biogenesis Principle: All living cells originate strictly from pre-existing, viable living cells through cellular division processes[cite: 8, 9, 16].
  • 4. Hereditary Continuity: Cells store genetic information encoded within DNA, which is accurately replicated and transmitted to daughter cells during division[cite: 7, 8, 9, 17].

Unit 3: Subcellular Organelles

The Endomembrane System

  • Nucleus: Encased in a double-membrane nuclear envelope embedded with nuclear pore complexes[cite: 37, 46, 83]. Stores chromatin (DNA-protein complex) and contains the nucleolus, the site of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) transcription and ribosome assembly[cite: 46, 83].
  • Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum (RER): Membranous network studded with membrane-bound ribosomes[cite: 37]. Synthesizes, folds, and provides quality control for membrane-bound and secreted proteins[cite: 37].
  • Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum (SER): Lacks attached ribosomes. Responsible for lipid synthesis (phospholipids and steroids), carbohydrate metabolism, and enzymatic detoxification of metabolic byproducts and toxins.
  • Golgi Apparatus: Series of flattened membranous cisternae displaying structural polarity (cis face to trans face)[cite: 37, 38]. Receives vesicles from the RER, modifies glycosylation patterns, and sorts molecules into transport or lysosomal vesicles[cite: 37, 38].
  • Lysosomes & Peroxisomes: Membrane-bound compartments housing hydrolytic enzymes for intracellular digestion (lysosomes) or oxidative enzymes for metabolic neutralization (peroxisomes).

Bioenergetic Organelles

  • Mitochondria: Double-membrane organelles possessing semi-autonomous circular DNA and 70S ribosomes[cite: 37, 38]. Features a smooth outer membrane and a folded inner membrane (cristae) optimizing surface area for electron transport chain complexes and ATP synthase[cite: 37, 38, 40]. Site of aerobic respiration[cite: 37, 38, 40].
  • Chloroplasts: Double-membrane structures in plant and algal cells containing thylakoids arranged in stacks (grana) surrounded by stroma. Contain chlorophyll pigments to capture light energy during photosynthesis.

Structural Framework

  • Microtubules: Cylindrical polymers composed of tubulin dimers. Facilitate intracellular vesicle transport via motor proteins, form centrioles and the mitotic spindle apparatus, and power cilia and flagella.
  • Microfilaments (Actin): Double-stranded helical polymers of actin. Responsible for cell motility, cortical tension, muscle contraction interaction, and forming the contractile ring during cytokinesis.
  • Intermediate Filaments: Tough, fibrous protein cables providing mechanical strength, maintaining cell shape, and anchoring the nucleus and organelles.

Unit 3: Plasma Membrane Transport

Mechanisms governing passive solute diffusion and active carrier-mediated transport.

Unit 3 Passive Transport Mechanisms

  • Simple Diffusion: Unassisted movement of small, nonpolar hydrophobic molecules (e.g., O2, CO2) directly across the phospholipid bilayer down a concentration gradient (high to low concentration).
  • Facilitated Diffusion: Passive movement of polar or charged solute molecules across the membrane mediated by channel or carrier proteins without cellular energy expenditure.
  • Osmosis: Net movement of water molecules across a selectively permeable membrane toward a higher solute concentration:
    • Hypertonic Solution: Higher solute concentration outside the cell → net water efflux (cell shrinks/plasmolyzes).
    • Hypotonic Solution: Lower solute concentration outside the cell → net water influx (cell swells/turgid).
    • Isotonic Solution: Equal solute concentration inside and outside → no net water movement.

Unit 3 Active & Bulk Transport Processes

  • Primary Active Transport: Direct consumption of ATP hydrolysis energy to transport molecules against their concentration gradient (low to high concentration).

    Sodium-Potassium Pump (Na+/K+ ATPase): Transports 3 Na+ ions out of the cell and 2 K+ ions into the cell per ATP consumed, maintaining cellular membrane potential.

  • Secondary Active Transport: Coupled transport utilizing the potential energy of an electrochemical gradient (established by primary active transport) to move a second solute against its gradient.
  • Bulk Transport:
    • Endocytosis: Vesicular uptake of macromolecules (Phagocytosis = particle uptake; Pinocytosis = fluid uptake; Receptor-Mediated = target ligand binding).
    • Exocytosis: Secretory vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane to export proteins and extracellular components.

Unit 3: Microscopy & Biological Discovery

Historical Developments in Cytology

  • Robert Hooke (1665): Examined cork sections under an early compound microscope and coined the term "cell" based on their structural appearance[cite: 81].
  • Antoine van Leeuwenhoek (1670s): Designed high-quality single-lens microscopes[cite: 82]. First to observe living microorganisms, including protozoa and bacteria ("animalcules")[cite: 82, 83].
  • Robert Brown (1831): Identified and named the cell nucleus while observing plant tissue[cite: 83].
  • Schleiden & Schwann (1838–1839): Proposed that plant tissues (Schleiden) and animal tissues (Schwann) are fundamentally composed of cells.

Unit 3/4: Bioenergetics

Pathways governing cellular energy transformation and ATP production.

Unit 4 Cellular Respiration Pathways

The aerobic catabolism of glucose into cellular energy (ATP) proceeds through three main stages:

  • 1. Glycolysis (Cytosol): Anaerobic breakdown of 6-carbon glucose into two 3-carbon pyruvate molecules. Yields a net 2 ATP (via substrate-level phosphorylation) and 2 NADH.
  • 2. Citric Acid Cycle (Mitochondrial Matrix): Oxidation of pyruvate to Acetyl-CoA followed by cyclic degradation. Yields 2 ATP, 6 NADH, and 2 FADH2, releasing CO2 as a byproduct.
  • 3. Oxidative Phosphorylation (Inner Mitochondrial Membrane): High-energy electrons from NADH and FADH2 power proton pumping across the inner membrane. Proton flow through ATP Synthase (chemiosmosis) yields ~26 to 28 ATP (Total yield: ~30-32 ATP).

Unit 3 Photosynthetic Pathways

  • Light-Dependent Reactions (Thylakoid Membrane): Photons excite chlorophyll reaction centers. Water photolysis generates oxygen gas, while electron transfer produces ATP and NADPH.
  • Calvin Cycle (Stroma): Carbon fixation pathway driven by the enzyme Rubisco. Utilizes ATP and NADPH to fix CO2 into G3P, generating precursor molecules for glucose synthesis.

Unit 4: The Cell Cycle & Control Points

Interphase & Regulatory Checkpoints

  • Interphase Stages:
    • G1 Phase (First Gap): Cellular growth, protein synthesis, and metabolic preparation[cite: 52].
    • S Phase (Synthesis): Replication of genomic DNA resulting in paired sister chromatids[cite: 52, 53].
    • G2 Phase (Second Gap): Final growth phase, organelle duplication, and preparation for nuclear division[cite: 53, 54].
  • Cell Cycle Checkpoints:
    • G1 Checkpoint (Restriction Point): Assesses nutrient supply, growth signals, and DNA integrity[cite: 58]. Unfavorable conditions trigger entry into non-dividing G0 state[cite: 58].
    • G2 Checkpoint: Verifies complete and error-free DNA replication prior to mitosis[cite: 58].
    • M Checkpoint (Spindle Assembly): Ensures proper attachment of kinetochores to spindle fibers before initiating anaphase chromosome separation[cite: 57, 59].

Unit 4: Mitosis & Cytokinesis

Nuclear division yielding two genetically identical diploid (2n) daughter nuclei.

  • Prophase / Prometaphase: Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes (sister chromatids bound at the centromere)[cite: 66]. The nucleolus disappears and the nuclear envelope breaks down[cite: 66]. Spindle fibers extend from centrosomes and attach to kinetochores[cite: 66].
  • Metaphase: Chromosomes align along the equatorial Metaphase Plate under tension from opposing kinetochore microtubules[cite: 66].
  • Anaphase: Proteolytic cleavage of cohesin proteins allows sister chromatids to separate[cite: 66]. Microtubules shorten, pulling daughter chromosomes to opposite spindle poles[cite: 66].
  • Telophase: Chromosomes reach poles and decondense back into chromatin[cite: 66]. Nuclear envelopes reassemble around each daughter genome, nucleoli reform, and spindle fibers disassemble[cite: 66].
  • Animal Cell Cytokinesis: A contractile ring of microfilaments (actin and myosin) pinches the plasma membrane inward, forming a Cleavage Furrow that separates the cell[cite: 66, 73].
  • Plant Cell Cytokinesis: Golgi-derived vesicles transport cell wall materials to the equator, coalescing to form a Cell Plate that develops into a dividing cell wall[cite: 66, 73].

Unit 4: Meiosis & Genetic Variation

Reduction Division Mechanics

Meiosis comprises two sequential nuclear divisions (Meiosis I and II) reducing a diploid (2n) germ cell into four distinct haploid (1n) gametes.

  • Meiosis I (Reductional Division):
    • Prophase I & Crossing Over: Homologous chromosomes pair via synapsis to form tetrads. Non-sister chromatids exchange genetic material at chiasmata, generating novel gene combinations.
    • Metaphase I & Independent Assortment: Homologous pairs align randomly in double rows along the metaphase plate.
    • Anaphase I / Telophase I: Homologous chromosomes separate to opposite poles (sister chromatids remain intact), reducing ploidy from diploid (2n) to haploid (1n).
  • Meiosis II (Equational Division): Sister chromatids separate in a sequence analogous to mitosis, yielding four unique haploid gametes.
